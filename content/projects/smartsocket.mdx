## Context

The Smart Socket is a wall socket that stops charging a device once its battery
is full, so the battery is never kept topped up and lasts longer. It is built
around an Arduino Uno with a current sensor and a relay, and it runs on real
230 V mains power. On test, it watched a laptop charge, noticed the charging
current tapering off near full, and cut the power.

You can watch and control the socket from an app on your phone, your computer
or the web. The apps talk to it over Bluetooth, showing its readings and
switching it on and off remotely.

## My role

This was a team project with ten people, and real-time embedded systems were new
to me. I built the apps that talk to the socket: the Android app, the Windows app
and the web app, including their Bluetooth connection to it. Later I polished all
three so they share the same screens, wording and behaviour, using an AI coding
assistant for that later work.

Teammates built the socket's hardware and firmware. I wrote much of the app code
on a teammate's computer, so many of those commits appear under his account in
the repository. The `stack` above lists only the app work, which is the same rule
every project on this site follows.

## Key decisions

**Simple, fast and working first.** Embedded systems were new to me, so for the
apps I aimed for screens that are simple, respond quickly and reliably do their
job, rather than piling on features.

**One app, three platforms.** The web, Android and Windows apps share the same
logic, screens and wording, so the socket behaves the same whichever one you
pick up. The web app reuses the phone app's logic and reaches the socket through
the browser's Web Bluetooth API, so it needs nothing installed.

**A simulator for working without the hardware.** Each app can run against a
simulated socket, so the screens could be built, tested and shown without the
real socket plugged in.

## The hard part

The hardest part was getting the socket and the apps to talk to each other:
reading the socket's measurements in the app, and switching the socket from the
app. At first I did not know what was going wrong.

After troubleshooting with my teammates, we found the cause was in the wiring.
The pins the components were connected to on the Arduino Uno had to match the
pins the code expected exactly, and the app had to reach those same pins. Once
the wiring, the code and the app agreed, the readings came through and remote
control worked.

## Outcome, and what I would do differently

It turned out well. The socket works on real mains power, and it can be watched
and controlled from all three apps.

If I did it again, I would add animations to make the apps more enjoyable to
use.
